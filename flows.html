      How to read this

      Direction
      Each band runs from the customer who pays to the supplier who is paid. Bands are gross payments: a dollar Microsoft pays Foxconn for an AI rack reappears in Foxconn's payment to NVIDIA, and again in NVIDIA's payment to TSMC. Column totals therefore overlap and should not be added together.
      Scope
      Every company's bands add up to its 2025 revenue. Coloured bands are payments between the 119 companies on the dashboard: chips, memory, wafers, tools, subsystems, materials, gases, IP, packaging, test and assembly. Grey shows money from customers without a tracked flow (mostly companies outside the 119, such as consumers, enterprises, Meta, Dell and Chinese fabs, plus purchases by tracked companies too small or uncertain to draw) and where the rest of each company's revenue goes: other suppliers and production costs, people and R&D, and operating profit. An operating loss appears as money drawn from reserves; when a profitable company’s tracked purchases exceed what the year’s costs absorbed (inventory, prepayments or equipment bought ahead of sales), the difference appears as purchases paid from cash. Where the split of a company’s remaining revenue between other suppliers, people and profit is still being researched, it is shown as one amount marked “breakdown pending”.
      Partial scopes
      Most of Amazon's, Alphabet's and Microsoft's revenue (retail, ads, software) and Apple's services revenue have little to do with chips. For these four, only the hardware part is counted: the hyperscalers' purchases of servers, networking and chips, and Apple's device sales ($307B) plus the $75B Foxconn pays Apple for components Apple buys and resells to it. Apple does not book those receipts as revenue, but its gross payment to Foxconn includes the parts, so both legs are shown. Most companies with large non-chip businesses, such as Linde, BASF, Canon or Kyocera, count only their chip-related segment. A few are shown whole because their other businesses trade with tracked companies: Samsung Electronics (phones and TVs), Samsung Electro-Mechanics (cameras and capacitors), Tata Electronics (iPhone assembly) and board makers such as Zhen Ding and AT&S. The company view states each scope and the full revenue.
      Market cap
      Market value is fetched live when the page opens, as on the companies page, and shown in tooltips and tables. It is a valuation of future profits, not money that moves in a year, so it is never drawn as a band. It values the whole company, while the revenue shown is often only the chip-related part, so the two should not be read as a price-to-sales ratio.
      Period
      Confidence
      Disclosed — the payer or supplier reports the amount or share for this counterparty (named, or identified with near certainty). Derived — calculated from reported figures plus a stated assumption. Estimated — analyst estimates or modelling from market shares; treat as an order of magnitude.
      Layout
      Tiers follow the dashboard. Tiers 10, 8, 7 and 0–1 are split by role (brands vs. ODMs, OSATs vs. test equipment, substrates vs. ABF film, wafers vs. polysilicon) because each contains a customer–supplier step of its own. Flows within a group, between groups drawn in the same column, or running against the chain (such as Samsung's phone business buying Snapdragon chips) are listed below the chart instead of being drawn.

      Every flow and every company account states its basis, with sources where a filing or report backs the figure; some estimates rest on modelling alone. These are static research figures; only market caps are live. Corrections are welcome.
